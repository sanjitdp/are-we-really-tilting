Are we really tilting?

The mechanics of reward guidance in flow and diffusion models

Sanjit Dandapanthula Nicholas M. Boffi
Carnegie Mellon University

Reward guidance algorithms steer a learned generative process toward the reward-tilted measure at inference time. While empirically powerful, these methods are prone to reward hacking: the guided model over-optimizes the reward at the cost of fidelity to the learned distribution. We show that reward hacking arises from an approximation made in most practical implementations of reward-guided diffusion — finite-particle plug-in estimation of the Doob h-function — even in the simplest non-trivial settings of Gaussian and Gaussian-mixture targets with quadratic rewards. In closed form, we isolate two distinct failure modes: within-mode reward hacking and the inability to select high-reward modes. We propose a closed-form reward damping schedule that corrects the within-mode bias with no additional compute, and clarify the role of best-of-n sampling in fixing mode selection. Experiments on Gaussian mixture targets, a 2D checkerboard, and FLUX.1 text-to-image generation confirm that our theoretical insights carry over to practical settings.

Background on reward guidance

Stochastic interpolants and flow matching

We assume access to a pre-trained flow model $b : [0,1] \times \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^d$. Samples from the data distribution $\rho_1 \in \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^d)$ are drawn by integrating the probability flow ODE

$$\dot{x}_t \;=\; b_t(x_t), \qquad x_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d),$$

until $t = 1$. The drift can be written in terms of the linear stochastic interpolant $I_t = (1-t)\,I_0 + t\,I_1$ (with $I_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)$ and $I_1 \sim \rho_1$ independent) as $b_t(x) = \mathbb{E}[\dot{I}_t \mid I_t = x]$, and is learned by minimizing the flow-matching objective (Lipman et al.; Albergo et al.).

For any noise schedule $\sigma_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times \ell}$, the forward SDE

$$dX_t \;=\; \Bigl( b_t(X_t) + \tfrac{1}{2}\,\sigma_t \sigma_t^{\!\top}\, \nabla \log \rho_t(X_t) \Bigr) dt \;+\; \sigma_t\, dB_t$$

shares the same time-marginals as the probability flow ODE.

Proof sketch · SDE matches the flow time-marginals

The Fokker–Planck equation for the SDE is $$\partial_t \rho_t \;=\; -\nabla \cdot (\rho_t b_t) - \tfrac{1}{2}\nabla^2\!:\!(\rho_t \sigma_t \sigma_t^{\!\top}) + \tfrac{1}{2} \nabla^2\!:\!(\rho_t \sigma_t \sigma_t^{\!\top}) \;=\; -\nabla \cdot (\rho_t b_t),$$ which is exactly the continuity equation for $\dot{x}_t = b_t(x_t)$. The two diffusion terms cancel, so the score-matching correction restores the ODE marginals while still letting us inject noise.

Reward guidance and the Doob h-transform

In reward-guided generation, we want samples not from $\rho_1$ but from the reward-tilted measure

$$\tilde{\rho}_1(x) \;\propto\; \rho_1(x)\, e^{\lambda r(x)},$$

for a reward $r : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}$ and inverse temperature $\lambda > 0$. This measure is the unique solution of a KL-regularized variational problem.

Proof sketch · variational characterization of the tilt

The reward-tilted measure solves $$\tilde{\rho}_1 \;=\; \arg\max_{\rho}\; \Bigl\{ \mathbb{E}_{X \sim \rho}[r(X)] - \tfrac{1}{\lambda}\,\mathrm{KL}(\rho \,\|\, \rho_1) \Bigr\}.$$ Writing the objective as an integral against $\rho_1$ in the density ratio $f = \rho/\rho_1$ and pointwise-maximizing with the constraint $\mathbb{E}_{X \sim \rho_1}[f(X)] = 1$ gives $f(x) \propto e^{\lambda r(x)}$ via a Lagrange multiplier, so $\tilde{\rho}_1(x) \propto \rho_1(x)\, e^{\lambda r(x)}$.

The Doob h-transform provides a principled way to reach $\tilde{\rho}_1$ at terminal time by modifying the drift of the forward SDE. Define the Doob h-function

$$h_t(x) \;\coloneq\; \mathbb{E}\!\left[ e^{\lambda r(X_1)} \,\big|\, X_t = x \right].$$

The guided ODE

$$\dot{\tilde{x}}_t \;=\; b_t(\tilde{x}_t) \;+\; \tfrac{1}{2}\, \sigma_t \sigma_t^{\!\top}\, \nabla \log h_t(\tilde{x}_t), \qquad \tilde{x}_0 = x_0,$$

then yields a sample $\tilde{x}_1 \sim \tilde{\rho}_1$ provided that $X_0$ and $X_1$ are independent. Domingo-Enrich et al. show that the memoryless schedule $\sigma_t = \sqrt{2(1-t)/t}\,I_d$ guarantees this property.

Plug-in estimation

The Doob h-function is intractable in general, so practitioners replace it with a $k$-particle plug-in estimator:

$$\hat{h}_t^{(k)}(x) \;=\; \frac{1}{k} \sum_{i=1}^{k} e^{\lambda r(X_1^{(i)})}, \qquad X_1^{(i)} \stackrel{\text{iid}}{\sim} p_{1\mid t}(\,\cdot\, \mid x).$$

In practice, $k$ is small for cost reasons. Our central claim is that, even with arbitrarily large $k$, this plug-in estimator does not produce samples from $\tilde{\rho}_1$ — it has two distinct biases that we characterize in closed form.

What we prove

Even in the simplest non-trivial settings — Gaussian and Gaussian-mixture targets with quadratic rewards — the plug-in estimator exhibits two distinct failure modes that are derivable in closed form:

  1. Within-mode reward hacking. The estimator over-rewards the model along directions of high reward, producing samples that drift off the data manifold even when only one mode is relevant.
  2. Mode-selection failure. The estimator has no mechanism to up-weight a distant high-reward mode against a closer low-reward mode — guidance cannot reweight the mixture.

We propose a closed-form reward damping schedule that fixes (1) at no extra compute, and show that best-of-n sampling is the natural fix for (2). See the paper for the precise statements and proofs.

Failure mode 1: within-mode reward hacking

On a single Gaussian target with a quadratic reward, the analytic tilt has a known closed form. The plug-in estimator overshoots it: samples concentrate too tightly and shift too far in the reward direction. Damping restores the analytic distribution.

Failure mode 2: mode selection

On a 1D Gaussian mixture with a step-function reward favouring the right mode, plug-in guidance cannot reweight the mixture toward the high-reward mode — trajectories follow whichever mode they were initialized near. Best-of-n resampling recovers the correct mixture weights.

Experiments

We confirm the theory on a 2D checkerboard target and on FLUX.1-dev with three reward families: a hand-crafted masked-intensity reward, a colour-channel reward, and the learned ImageReward human-preference reward.
